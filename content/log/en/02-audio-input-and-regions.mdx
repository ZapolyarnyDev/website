## How sound enters the field

After the initial idea, an uncomfortable question quickly came up: how should sound even enter such an
environment?

The simplest route would be to take audio and feed it directly into a response generator. But then
the research quickly turns into an ordinary, boring audio effect. I wanted something a little different: sound should
only act on the field, and the entire subsequent response should pass through the environment's internal
dynamics.

That's why the project gained an input drive signal layer (also known as drive).

## Field regions

The field is divided into three regions:

- `input` — the left part of the field, where the external signal enters;
- `assoc` — the central associative region;
- `output` — the right part of the field, from which a response can later be read.

This is a very rough scheme, but it is convenient for the first checks. If the signal really affects
the environment, we can see whether a change appears not only in the input region but farther along:
in the associative and output regions.

At this stage, biological plausibility is not important to me. What matters is being able to test the entire scheme I came up with:
information enters the input -> associative region -> reaches the output -> metrics are written to directories.

## Synthetic input

Before live audio, it is useful to have a controlled stimulus. That's what synthetic input is for:
a sine wave, pulses or noise. Such a signal is easier to repeat, compare and debug.

For example, pulse mode provides a clear scenario: the field warms up a little, then a stimulus is applied
to the input region, and we can watch how far it travels.

Without controlled input, it would be harder for me to tell whether the field responds to sound or its output is entirely independent of it.

## WAV input

For regular audio, the project extracts several features from a WAV:

- RMS, meaning the frame's loudness;
- onset, meaning abrupt changes in the spectrum;
- spectral centroid, roughly speaking, the shift of energy toward higher or lower frequencies.

These features are mixed into a single `drive` value. Then `drive` becomes a phase impulse
applied to the input region.

There is an important limitation here: the field does not receive words. It does not know where a syllable, word or command is.
It receives only a numerical stimulus built from acoustic features.

## Why a phase impulse

This kind of input fits the oscillator model well. Each point has a phase, and an external signal can
shift that phase slightly. Then neighboring couplings, local energy and plasticity determine
whether the change stays local or starts to spread farther.

I like this scheme because it is simple enough to debug, yet
not entirely trivial. The same input can produce different results depending on the field's current
state.

## What needs to be checked here

There are several practical questions for this part of the project:

- how strong the drive signal should be;
- whether the associative region needs additional excitation along with input;
- how quickly the output region responds to input;
- what changes with voice, silence and synthetic pulses;
- whether the system turns into self-exciting noise too easily.
